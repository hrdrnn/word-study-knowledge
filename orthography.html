Orthography

Orthography concerns the conventional written representation of words. A grapheme is a letter or group of letters representing a sound in a particular word.

Common patterns

Final silent e
In make and hope, final e helps signal a long vowel. Before some vowel-initial suffixes it is dropped: make → making. Exceptions and other functions exist.
Consonant doubling
In a stressed short-vowel syllable ending in a single consonant, a final consonant may double before a vowel-initial suffix: stop → stopping; begin → beginning. Compare open → opening.
Consonant plus y
Final y often changes to i before certain suffixes: happy → happiness; carry → carried. It normally remains before -ing: carrying.
Digraphs and trigraphs
Two or three letters can represent one phoneme: sh in ship, ch in chin, igh in night. A letter group can have different pronunciations in different words.
Vowel teams
Groups such as ai in rain, ee in green and oa in boat can represent a vowel sound. These patterns have exceptions.
Morphological spelling
Related words may preserve a meaningful spelling even when sounds change: sign and signature; heal and health.

Use Australian conventions, such as colour, centre and organise. Explain only patterns that genuinely apply to the target word.
